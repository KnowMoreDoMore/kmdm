    A small glossary

    AI, machine learning, and generative AI

    AI is a broad field. Machine learning fits patterns from data. Generative AI creates outputs such as text or images.

    Model, token, and context

    A model is a learned computational system. Tokens are units used to process text. Context is the input available for a particular response.

    Hallucination

    A plausible output that is inaccurate or unsupported. Check claims against original sources. IBM explainer ↗

    RAG and agents

    Retrieval-augmented generation supplies documents as context. An agent uses a model alongside tools to take steps toward a goal. Neither guarantees correctness.

    Open weights, open source, and local models

    Open weights means model parameters are available. Open source also concerns licensing and other components. Local means running on your hardware; check network settings, licenses, and data handling.

    Misconception: a high rank means it is reliable

    A ranking reflects its evaluation method and participants. Test your actual task, factual accuracy, accessibility, and privacy needs. Explore Arena ↗

    Model literacy

    Where a model runs. What you can change.

    A model, the app around it, and the service hosting it are different parts of a system. Check the exact version and configuration before making a choice.

    Hosted, local, and mixed setups

    Hosted

    The provider runs the model on remote computers. Your request reaches that service. Check account terms, retention, sharing, access controls, and pricing. The app may also connect to other tools.

    Local

    Inference runs on your device or a server you manage. You take responsibility for installation, memory, updates, and access. Check whether the surrounding app still uses remote services or stores sensitive logs.

    Mixed

    A local interface can call a cloud model, retrieve web content, or use remote tools. The location of the interface does not establish the location of processing. Trace the full data path.

    Concrete example: Ollama documents both local use and cloud features, with a local-only setting that disables its cloud models and web search. This does not establish the behavior of every app connected to Ollama. Read Ollama’s FAQ ↗

    Open weights, open source, and licenses

    What does “open weights” give you?

    Weights are the learned numerical parameters. Downloadable weights can let you run a model with compatible software. Check the license for permission to use, modify, or share them. Availability alone does not supply training code or data information.

    What does “open source AI” mean?

    Under the Open Source Initiative’s definition 1.0, the system must support freedoms to use, study, modify, and share. It also needs data information, code, and parameters in forms suitable for modification. “Open” is used differently by different publishers; identify the definition and terms being claimed. OSI definition ↗

    What should I read before using a model?

    Read the exact model card and license, including version, intended uses, limitations, evaluation, and restrictions. Check the software license separately. A model family name does not establish every release’s license or suitability. Hugging Face model-card guide ↗

    Read an Arena ranking carefully

    Standard Arena rankings aggregate preferences from head-to-head comparisons. Inspect the task category, model version, vote count, confidence interval, and rank spread. A relative score is not a percentage of correct answers. Arena’s leaderboard guide ↗

    Arena’s factuality view combines preferences with checks of web-verifiable claims. Read which view is selected and how it is scored. This still does not certify an answer, prove privacy, or validate use in your classroom. Factuality methodology ↗

    Why hardware requirements vary

    Model weights need storage and working memory. Runtime overhead, context, and concurrent requests need additional memory. Ollama supports CPU, GPU, and mixed loading; compatibility depends on hardware and software. Hardware support ↗ · Memory and concurrency FAQ ↗

    Quantization stores weights at lower precision to reduce memory. Quality and speed depend on the method, model, and hardware. Test the resulting model on your task. Hugging Face quantization guide ↗

    Explore weight storage

    A small comparison before committing

    Try the same representative tasks and check accuracy, corrections needed, latency, accessibility, and cost. Include privacy and license requirements in the comparison. Use public or fictional test material. A larger model or higher rank does not settle suitability.

    Teacher support and student learning

    A draft lesson or grant outline can help a teacher begin a task. For a student practicing a skill, decide which thinking and practice the student needs to do themselves. A useful output alone does not answer that question.

    Possible teacher uses

    Slide 20 suggests brainstorming, grant writing, lesson planning, standards alignment, differentiation, and flipped-learning supports. Treat these as candidate uses to evaluate in your setting.

    Brainstorming and lesson drafts

    Start with a learning goal and public or fictional context. Compare draft ideas with your own plan, check the content, and revise for the students you teach.

    Grant outlines and standards alignment

    Supply the official requirements or standards. Check every proposed match, citation, budget figure, and eligibility claim against those documents before using a draft.

    Differentiation and flipped-learning supports

    Review reading level, correctness, accessibility, and cultural fit. Plan a way to observe student understanding, and offer a route that does not require an AI account.

    Choose a level of engagement

    Consider a limited trial, teacher-only use, a supported student activity, or declining use. Explain the reason, the alternatives, and the evidence that would lead you to revisit the decision.

    Six risks and misconceptions

    Expand a topic for evidence, limits, and a question to take into a decision. The misconception statements are examples of claims to examine, not survey findings about what everyone believes.

    Unreliable outputs

    Misconception to examine: A fluent answer with citations must be correct.

    What the source says: NIST describes confabulation: confidently presented false content. A reference in an answer still needs verification. NIST GenAI Profile, 2024 · sections 2.2 and 3 ↗

    Limits and uncertainty: Error rates depend on the task, model, and evaluation conditions. A good demonstration cannot establish reliability in every setting.

    A practical check: Open the cited source and check that it supports the exact claim. Recalculate important numbers. Identify who reviews errors before anyone acts.

    Privacy and data use

    Misconception to examine: A training opt-out settles every privacy concern.

    What the source says: NIST identifies leakage, unauthorized disclosure, and re-identification as privacy risks. Training is only one part of data handling. NIST GenAI Profile, 2024 · section 2.4 ↗

    Limits and uncertainty: Retention, sharing, access, and deletion need separate checks for the specific service and account.

    A practical check: Read the relevant settings and terms. Ask where prompts, uploads, logs, and outputs go. Use public or fictional examples when possible.

    Bias and representation

    Misconception to examine: Using software removes human bias.

    What the source says: NIST identifies historical bias and performance differences across groups or languages. Aggregate scores can conceal those differences. NIST GenAI Profile, 2024 · sections 2.6 and 3 ↗

    Limits and uncertainty: Results may change with population, context, and the fairness measure used.

    A practical check: Look for evaluation with affected people. Ask which groups were tested, what failed, and how someone can challenge a harmful result.

    Labor and working conditions

    Misconception to examine: Exposure to AI means those jobs will disappear.

    What the source says: The ILO’s 2025 study estimates which occupational tasks could be affected. It expects job transformation to be more likely than full replacement because many tasks still require human input. ILO Working Paper 140, May 2025 · research estimate ↗

    Limits and uncertainty: Exposure estimates are not counts of actual layoffs. Adoption, job design, and policy influence outcomes; displacement and changes in work quality remain concerns.

    A practical check: Ask which tasks change, who receives the time savings, and whether staff have a voice, training, and workable expectations.

    Environmental impacts

    Misconception to examine: One per-prompt figure tells us AI’s environmental cost.

    What the source says: The IEA’s 2025 analysis models electricity demand from data centers and shows that local impacts can differ from global averages. NIST also identifies energy and water used in training and operation as environmental concerns. IEA Energy and AI, April 2025 · estimates and scenarios ↗
    NIST GenAI Profile, 2024 · section 2.5

    Limits and uncertainty: A prompt estimate depends on workload and system boundaries. Electricity projections depend on adoption and efficiency. They do not establish every site’s water use, emissions, or community effects.

    A practical check: Ask what was measured, where, and when. Compare a specific proposal’s power, water, noise, and land impacts with alternatives. Follow DMV infrastructure records →

    Copyright and authorship

    Misconception to examine: Writing a prompt automatically gives me copyright in its output.

    What the source says: The U.S. Copyright Office’s January 2025 report says protection depends on sufficient human expressive authorship. AI assistance does not automatically disqualify human-created work; merely providing prompts is insufficient. U.S. Copyright Office, January 2025 · U.S. scope ↗

    Limits and uncertainty: Copyrightability, permission to use material, attribution, and academic integrity are different questions. Application depends on the work and jurisdiction; the report does not decide your particular case.

    A practical check: Record your own contribution and disclose assistance where required. Check source permissions and licenses separately from a tool’s account terms.

    Evidence before enthusiasm—or alarm

    Evaluate an AI claim.

    A practical set of questions for a product pitch, a headline, or a community proposal. You can choose to adopt, test, limit, or decline a use.


      Check a box after discussing a question, even if the answer remains unknown. Checks record your progress, not the quality of the evidence. They reset when you change scenarios or reload. No answers are collected.

      Separate a claim from its evidence

      Write down the specific claim, who made it, the date, and the population or place it concerns. Find the original document. Look for independent evidence and the strongest credible counterargument.

      Keep uncertainty visible

      “Unknown,” “not measured,” and “disputed” are useful findings. A successful demonstration does not establish performance in your setting. An advocacy position or company statement is a perspective to examine.

      Questions can change the decision

      What evidence would change your mind? What would justify a limited trial, further review, or stopping? Who has the authority to decide, and how can affected people be heard?
